# Script Stop 02: Adelaide House: Heat, Medicine, and Distance

Draft script for the Alice Springs / Mparntwe walking guide. This is the second test stop after the Mparntwe field briefing.

## Revised Script

Stand outside Adelaide House Museum at 48 Todd Street. Face the two-storey stone building, verandahs, roofline, and nearby John Flynn Memorial Church. It looks more like a large inland house than a hospital. In Alice Springs in 1926, medicine began by making a room survivable.

At Alice Springs Airport, January's mean maximum is 36.6 degrees Celsius. The annual mean maximum is 28.9. In an average year, the town records more than 175 days above 30 degrees, 93 above 35, and 17 above 40. The record high is 45.7. Mean annual rainfall is about 284 millimetres, and winter nights can fall below freezing. A hospital here had to manage heat, dust, cold, isolation, and the uneven arrival of rain.

Adelaide House was designed by Reverend John Flynn for the Australian Inland Mission and opened as the first hospital in Alice Springs. It served as the region's only hospital until 1939. Illness might begin at a pastoral station, railway camp, police patrol, mission outpost, or town verandah. By the time a patient reached this block on Todd Street, the disease or injury had already travelled through hours, sometimes days, of weather and track.

Look first at the verandahs. They hold shade away from the rooms and widen the liveable edge of the building. The stone walls add thermal mass. The ventilated roof and cellar-cooling system pulled air below ground, cooled it, ducted it into the rooms, and drew it out through the upper storey. The method belonged to the same practical world as the Coolgardie safe, where wet hessian and moving air kept food from spoiling before refrigeration was common.

The hospital also generated its own electricity. In 1926, the Northern Territory was administered by the Commonwealth, Alice Springs was still thinly served by public infrastructure, and mission organisations often provided services that government had not built at urban scale. Power gave Adelaide House light, mechanical capacity, and a place for radio experiments. The building had to supply part of the city it did not yet have around it.

Flynn stands behind the project, while Alfred Traeger gives this stop its human scene. Traeger came from South Australia, where he studied mechanical and electrical engineering at the School of Mines from 1912. He worked for the Metropolitan Tramways Trust, the Postmaster-General's Department, and Hannan Bros in Adelaide, repairing car generators and electrical equipment. His qualifications were practical: weak power, damaged machines, improvised circuits, and the cost of making equipment simple.

Flynn hired Traeger in 1926 for Northern Territory radio experiments at 500 pounds a year. One story from the beginning catches the scale of the enterprise. Flynn bought a generator from him; Traeger asked 29 pounds, 10 shillings because 30 pounds sounded too high. Flynn later said he would have paid 50. The bargain is almost comic, but it belongs to the history of this building. Inland medicine was being assembled from a minister's ambition, an engineer's workshop habits, a generator, and a continent-sized communications problem.

Now look for the engine room. On 25 November 1926, Traeger made one of Australia's first successful outback field-radio telegram receptions and transmissions from Adelaide House. Picture the experiment inside the stone shell: an engine running, a transmitter drawing limited power, wires carried through a hospital building, and operators waiting for a signal strong enough to be useful. Outside were Todd Street, the Todd River, the ranges, and hundreds of kilometres of tracks where a patient could be out of sight long before they were out of danger.

Traeger worked on sequence. A nurse or station resident had to report symptoms before a doctor could advise treatment. The doctor had to know the location before transport could be sent. A radio set that was costly, fragile, heavy, or difficult to operate would fail at the edge of the system, where it was needed most. Traeger's later pedal radio became famous because it answered a severe design brief: cheap, durable, small, low-powered, and usable by people far from technical help.

Adelaide House also records the boundaries of care. In February 1934, the federal government decided Aboriginal people could no longer use the hospital. A galvanised-iron hut was built on the east bank of the Todd River for Aboriginal patients. Keep that geography in mind as you face the stone building: institutional care on Todd Street, displaced care across the river, and a Commonwealth policy that sorted patients by race while using the language of inland service.

Before you leave, let your eyes move from stone wall to verandah, from roofline to the church beside it, and then toward the Todd River. Adelaide House cooled air, generated power, housed patients, and tested radio because distance made medicine mechanical. Its walls also marked who could enter that system and who was pushed outside it. Then turn toward the John Flynn Memorial Church, where a practical inland network becomes memorial architecture.
