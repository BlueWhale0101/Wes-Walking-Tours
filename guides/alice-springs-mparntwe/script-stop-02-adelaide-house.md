Stop 2: Adelaide House — Heat, Medicine, and Distance

Stand outside Adelaide House at 48 Todd Street. Face the two-storey stone building: the deep verandahs, high roofline, and the John Flynn Memorial Church immediately beside it. Adelaide House opened in 1926 as Alice Springs’ first hospital and remained the region’s only hospital until 1939. From the street, it has the scale of a substantial inland house, with shade built around its rooms.

In the cooler months Alice Springs can seem almost gentle. Adelaide House was built for the other seasons as well. Summer days above 35 degrees were routine, with the fiercest spells pushing toward 45. Winter could reverse the problem: mornings fell to freezing and below. Most years brought less than 300 millimetres of rain. Then a storm could break over country dry for months, sending water through channels and across the Todd River floodplain on which the town developed. Heat, dust, cold, sudden floodwater, and long distances entered the daily work of a hospital: how patients travelled, what food kept, whether a person could sleep, and how staff worked through an afternoon.

Look along the verandahs. Their width holds direct sun off the walls and windows, widening the liveable edge of the building. The stone walls add thermal mass, slowing the day’s heat as it moves inward. Above, the roof was ventilated. Beneath the building, a cellar-cooling system brought incoming air through an underground space, ducted it into the rooms, and drew it upward through the upper storey. The method belonged to the same practical world as the Coolgardie safe, where wet hessian and moving air kept food from spoiling before refrigeration was common.

The hospital also generated its own electricity. Adelaide House was the first building in Alice Springs to have it. Power gave the rooms light and mechanical capacity, and made an engine room available for radio experiments. A medical service beyond the town needed a way to hear from people who could not reach Todd Street quickly.

Flynn stood behind the project; Alfred Traeger made its radio equipment work. Traeger trained at the South Australian School of Mines from 1912, then repaired generators and electrical equipment for the Metropolitan Tramways Trust, the Postmaster-General’s Department, and Hannan Brothers in Adelaide. He knew what weak power, damaged machinery, improvised connections, and scarce spare parts did to an otherwise good design.

Flynn hired Traeger in 1926 for Northern Territory radio experiments. On 25 November, Traeger received and transmitted a field-radio telegram from Adelaide House’s engine room, one of Australia’s first successful outback radio experiments. The engine room is not visible from Todd Street, but this stone structure held an engine, transmitter, wires, and operators waiting for a usable signal. Beyond the town lay stations, camps, river crossings, and long stretches of track where a sick or injured person could be days from the nearest doctor.

Over the next two years, the problem was at the far end of the line. Sets were cumbersome and expensive, power was scarce, and a station family could not be expected to maintain specialist equipment or send Morse accurately in an emergency. Traeger returned to Adelaide to make a smaller, cheaper set for an untrained operator.

In 1928 he coupled a transmitter to a generator driven by bicycle pedals. The operator could make electricity with their feet while keeping both hands free for the key. The first pedal wireless went to Augustus Downs Station, 240 kilometres north of Cloncurry. On 19 June 1929, the station manager’s wife, Gertrude Rothery, sent its first message to Traeger’s assistant at the base. She meant to send, “Hello, Harry.” Her Morse arrived as, “O hell, O hell, O Harry.”

In 1931 Traeger added a typewriter-like keyboard that converted typed words into Morse signals. A station resident could report symptoms; a doctor could give immediate advice, decide whether treatment could be managed locally, and establish where transport would need to go. The pedal radio’s power source, controls, and message system were designed for people without electrical training or reliable mains power.

Adelaide House also records the boundaries of care. In February 1934, the federal government decided Aboriginal people could no longer use the hospital. A galvanised-iron hut was built on the east bank of the Todd River for Aboriginal patients. Keep that geography in mind as you face the stone building: institutional care on Todd Street, displaced care across the river, and a Commonwealth policy that sorted patients by race while using the language of inland service.

Before moving on, look again from the shaded verandah to the roofline, then to the church beside the building. Adelaide House cooled air, generated electricity, admitted patients, and tested radio equipment. The John Flynn Memorial Church, opened in 1956, is the later memorial beside the machinery, planning, and unequal access that shaped the town’s first inland hospital.
