# Script Stop 01: Field Briefing: A Town Inside Country

Draft script for the Alice Springs / Mparntwe walking guide. This is the first test stop for style review before scripting the rest of the route.

## Revised Script

Stand in Todd Mall near Parsons Street, under the Yeperenye Moth Shade Structures. Look up at the laser-cut steel panels and the line of shade running toward the Todd River. Alice Springs Town Council says these structures were completed in 2013 for the CBD Revitalisation project. Susan Dugdale designed them; Pip McManus produced the panels. Start with the fact above your head: in Alice Springs, shade is infrastructure. It decides how far people walk, where shops can trade, and how long a public place can function.

The town you see was built inside an older country, not on vacant ground. Alice Springs Town Council acknowledges the Central Arrernte people as Traditional Owners of Mparntwe, the Country where Alice Springs sits. Lhere Artepe Aboriginal Corporation identifies the Mparntwe, Irlpme, and Antulye estate groups as native title holders for Alice Springs. Long before surveyors drew streets here, Arrernte people knew this country through named sites, water, law, kinship, and responsibility. The town grid came later; it did not erase the country beneath it.

The shade structures make that older geography visible in the commercial centre. Council says the moth forms refer to three sacred caterpillars of Mparntwe: Ayepe-arenye, Ntyarlke, and Utnerrengatye. Todd Mall is easy to misread as an ordinary retail strip. The public art says otherwise: this pavement carries Arrernte knowledge through a corridor now managed for tourists, shoppers, and council crews.

The British did not establish Alice Springs because the interior was empty. They came because country with water and range gaps could carry a telegraph line. In March 1871, W. W. Mills reached a waterhole while surveying the telegraph route. He named it Alice Springs after Alice Todd, wife of telegraph chief Sir Charles Todd. Alice Todd never visited the station, and Mills had not found springs. He had found water in a usually dry river system and mistaken it for a permanent spring. The town's English name begins with a small absurdity: neither Alice nor springs were in Alice Springs.

A telegraph line could not survive as a line alone. It needed repeater stations, repair crews, police protection, stores, and dependable water. That is why a named waterhole became strategic in the colonial imagination. The first European logic of Alice Springs was not tourism or lifestyle. It was maintenance. A fragile communications system crossed a desert, and every working part needed people stationed near water.

The town centre still did not begin with the name Alice Springs. Government Surveyor David Lindsay surveyed the township in 1888 and named it Stuart after John McDouall Stuart, whose 1861-62 expedition mapped a south-north route toward the coast. Arltunga gold, found in 1887 about 110 kilometres east, gave the settlement another function. Gold brought traffic a telegraph station alone would not have produced: miners seeking claims, carriers moving equipment, police handling disputes, and merchants selling food to people who could not grow it.

For nearly half a century, the names did not line up. The waterhole was Alice Springs. The surveyed township was Stuart. The railway and post office used Alice Springs before the town did. The town was officially gazetted as Alice Springs on 31 August 1933. That confusion is evidence. The place was assembled by systems arriving out of sequence: exploration naming, telegraph construction, gold traffic, rail freight, postal administration, and finally a legal town name.

Those systems left buildings you will meet on this walk. Stuart Town Gaol was completed in 1909 after the earlier Heavitree Gap gaol was judged inadequate and flood-prone. Adelaide House opened in 1926 as the town's first hospital. The railway reached Stuart in 1929. A post office called Alice Springs opened in Railway Terrace in 1932, one year before the town itself was renamed. Alice Springs became a town when distance generated institutions. People needed custody, medicine, schooling, freight, land records, and courts because alternatives were too far away.

Look east along the mall toward the Todd River. Susan Dugdale & Associates says the 2013 CBD work reopened the northern end of Todd Mall to traffic and strengthened the link between the CBD and the river. The design used endemic planting, wide paving, brick benches, and the moth shade structures above you. It also removed an older sail-shade structure and rotunda to restore sightlines between a significant sacred river red gum and sister trees at the river edge. A single planning project had to serve traffic, pedestrians, sacred trees, public safety, retail trade, and the pull of the river.

The mall is being reassigned again. In 2026, ABC reported a $20 million Todd Mall redevelopment replacing red bricks with white pavers, removing 12 trees, planting 20, and adding new shade, lighting, toilets, bubblers, and public art. Nearby Yeperenye Shopping Centre had a separate $27 million plan with more than 200 car spaces, office space, and a rooftop restaurant. The materials have changed, but the problem has not: a desert town still has to make commerce, water, heat, movement, and public life work on the same ground.

Carry that problem through the walk. At each stop, ask what distance forced people to build, what water allowed them to attempt, and what Arrernte country already held before the colonial institution arrived. Then walk toward Adelaide House. The next stop is a two-storey stone hospital built for a town where illness, childbirth, surgery, radio contact, heat, and hundreds of kilometres of track converged in one small building.
