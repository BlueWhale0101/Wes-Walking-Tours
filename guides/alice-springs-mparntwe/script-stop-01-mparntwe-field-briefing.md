Stop 1: Mparntwe — A Town Inside Country

Stand in Todd Mall at the corner of Parsons Street, beneath the Yeperenye Moth Shade Structures. Look up at the steel frames, the cut panels, and the coloured undersides. Depending on the sun, their shadows spread across the paving as moth wings. The structures were completed in 2013 for the CBD revitalisation project. Susan Dugdale of SDA designed them; Pip McManus made the plasma-cut steel panels.

Begin with the shade. In Alice Springs, it determines which side of a street people choose, where they stop, and how long a bench or shopfront remains usable in the afternoon. These structures are municipal infrastructure—steel, polyurethane finish, foundations, maintenance crews—but their forms carry an older local geography. At the apex, sunlight can pick out the Arrernte names Ayeperenye, Ntyarlke, and Utnerrengatye on the rusted support beams.

Mparntwe is the Arrernte name for the Alice Springs area. Alice Springs Town Council acknowledges Central Arrernte people as the Traditional Owners of this Country, and Lhere Artepe Aboriginal Corporation represents the native-title holders of the Mparntwe, Irlpme, and Antulye estate groups. The caterpillar forms above you refer to ancestral beings associated with the creation stories of this landscape. The structures do not explain that knowledge; they mark its authorised public presence in a commercial street.

That order is worth keeping in view for the rest of the walk. The hospital, church, courthouse, gaol, school, and railway buildings ahead were built by colonial and later Australian institutions. They came into country already known through law, kinship, water, and responsibility. The town grid did not begin the history of this place. It imposed another set of names, materials, boundaries, and official functions upon it.

The English name came from a communications project that had first to solve a physical problem: how to get wagons, poles, wire, batteries, horses, food, and men through the MacDonnell Ranges. The Overland Telegraph Line was built in three sections, driving north, south, and through the centre of the continent. The southern work progressed comparatively smoothly. In the central section, the ranges stopped the survey parties. John Ross’s advance work had not found a route suitable for wagons.

In February 1871, Gilbert McMinn identified a passage through Simpson’s Gap. W. W. Mills then followed the Todd River north-east through the range country. In March he reached a waterhole and named it Alice Springs after Alice Todd, wife of the South Australian Superintendent of Telegraphs, Sir Charles Todd. Alice Todd never visited. Local histories record that Mills had mistaken a waterhole in a usually dry river system for a permanent spring. The name endured, including its small factual defect.

The waterhole had a practical value greater than the name. A telegraph station needed people who could stay: operators, repair workers, stockmen, stores, batteries, horses, and a reliable place to draw water. The completed line would carry electrical pulses along a single wire between Adelaide and Darwin, linking Australia to the undersea cable network and the rest of the world. A broken insulator, storm damage, a failed battery, or a damaged pole could interrupt that chain. Water made this point in Arrernte country useful to a colonial maintenance system.

The settlement beside the station was not initially Alice Springs. In 1888, government surveyor David Lindsay laid out a township and named it Stuart, after John McDouall Stuart, whose 1861–62 expedition had traced a route across the continent. The names ran alongside each other for decades: Alice Springs for the waterhole and telegraph station; Stuart for the surveyed town.

Gold at Arltunga, about 110 kilometres east, gave the settlement another role. Gold was discovered there in 1887, bringing miners, carriers, merchants, police, equipment, and supplies through Stuart. The railway reached the town in 1929. A post office named Alice Springs opened in Railway Terrace in 1932, while the town itself was still officially Stuart. On 31 August 1933, the township was finally gazetted as Alice Springs.

Those dates describe a town assembled in layers. A waterhole became a telegraph station; a surveyed township took another man’s name; gold traffic increased its importance; rail and postal administration adopted the name that the town later inherited. The institutions on this walk followed. Stuart Town Gaol was completed in 1909. Adelaide House opened in 1926 as the first hospital. Courts, schools, freight yards, churches, and government offices were ways of keeping people, supplies, records, and authority in a place far from the larger capitals that directed them.

Now look east along the mall, toward the Todd River. The 2013 redesign used wide paving, brick benches, endemic planting, and these shade structures to reconnect the commercial centre with the river corridor. It also reopened sightlines to a significant river red gum and related trees near the river edge. The same block has to accommodate desert heat, pedestrian movement, cars, retail trade, public art, and places of continuing cultural significance.

Walk south-west along Todd Mall toward Todd Street, then turn to Adelaide House. Its deep verandahs and stone walls belong to the next part of this story: a hospital built to keep patients, staff, machinery, and medical messages functioning through heat, distance, floodwater, and unreliable transport.
